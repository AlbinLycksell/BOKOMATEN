# Swichboard — Designspråk
## Verkstad: a design language for the digital tradesman

## 1. The vision in one paragraph

Swichboard is the digital colleague Magnus didn't know he could afford. The brand has to feel like that — earned, dignified, quietly competent. Not a Stockholm startup wearing a hi-vis vest as a costume. Not a consumer app pretending to do trades. Something that belongs on the same workbench as a Bahco-skiftnyckel and a Hilti-borrmaskin: built by people who understand the work, finished to a higher standard than the job requires, designed to outlast the next tool fashion. The brand's job is to make a 47-year-old VVS-företagare in Bromma look at it and think, _"Det här ser ut som något jag redan använder."_

---

## 2. The design language is called Verkstad

_Verkstad_ — Swedish for workshop. Not a metaphor. The actual place where tools meet material, where knowledge gets transferred from master to lärling, where a job is finished or it isn't. The word carries weight in Swedish; it speaks to seriousness, competence, and the dignity of practical work. It's the right name for the design language because every choice in the system has to answer to it: would this look at home on the workbench, or would it look out of place there?

Verkstad is built from three intersecting influences, each load-bearing.

### 2.1 From Pleo — the editorial Scandinavian baseline

Pleo's marketing surfaces are a masterclass in restraint. The display headline ("_Låt utgifterna lyfta ditt företagande_") sits in the optical center of an almost empty composition. The product screenshot doesn't float in a void — it lives among real objects: a glass of water, a brass-faced clock, an amber kettle, a calculator. The photography says: this product is part of the actual life of a real Swedish business owner, sitting on the same morning desk where the coffee cools.

Swichboard borrows three things from Pleo, directly:

**Editorial composition over feature-list density.** Marketing pages feel like a magazine article, not a product spec. One headline, one hero image, one decision. Density only when the user has earned it — deep documentation, integration pages, the API reference. The homepage shows what the product feels like, not what it does.

**Photography as the brand.** Real Swedish hantverker-life. A hand resting on a worn van steering wheel. A phone in a magnetic holder on the dashboard, screen lit with a Swichboard notification. An Apple Watch face visible against a navy Snickers-jacket sleeve, the orange Swichboard pip on the screen. A morning kök i Skellefteå at 06:30 with frost on the window. Never stock photography. Never models pretending to be electricians. The photographer brief: shoot the actual customers, with their actual tools, in their actual lives. The brand becomes recognizable from across the room because the photography is so specifically Swedish-trades that it cannot be confused with anything else.

**The pill button.** Pleo's rounded buttons are signature. Swichboard adopts them: generous corner radius, honest borders, primary in solid navy or signaloranje, secondary in outline-only. Same shape and proportions across web, native iOS, native Android, and SMS-link landing pages. One pill, everywhere.

### 2.2 From Klarna — the bold layout system

Klarna's brand guidelines are unembarrassed about it: "Scale headlines as big as possible, and maybe add a playful touch by offsetting a word or line to create tension." The grid is rigorous: a 6% margin of the shortest side as standard, 12% in double-margin contexts, gutters always half of the margin size, trademark width at 1/3 of the format's shortest side. Layouts are type-led OR image-led — never both fighting for attention. Off-white preferred, occasionally Black or Klarna Pink for extra impact and contrast.

Swichboard borrows three things from Klarna, structurally:

**The grid discipline.** Verkstad adopts the same 6% / 12% / half-gutter / one-third-trademark system. This is not a suggestion. It is the rule that allows a junior designer in 2027 to produce a marketing asset that sits comfortably next to one a senior designer made in 2026. The grid does the heavy lifting; the brand becomes consistent without anyone having to think about consistency.

**The type-led layout.** When the message is _"Magnus missar inga akutsamtal"_, that's the entire layout. Type goes large, breathes, sits left-aligned in the grid, allows itself an offset word for tension. No supporting illustration. No product screenshot below it. The headline _is_ the design. This direction unlocks an entire category of marketing surfaces — outdoor (job-site billboard near Nordbygg), social cards, sales-deck section dividers — that Pleo's photography-led system can't reach affordably.

**One signal color, used with conviction.** Klarna's pink is Klarna. Swichboard's signal color is **signaloranje** — the high-vis orange of Swedish hi-vis vests, road-work signage, life jackets, snow-plow blades, and crucially, the housings of Husqvarna and Stihl tools. It signals attention and safety, both of which are at the core of what the product does. Used in the same proportion that Klarna uses pink: rare, but unforgettable. The CTA button. The emergency state. The wordmark accent. Never as decoration, always as signal.

### 2.3 From Swedish industrial heritage — the honest tool

This is the influence that distinguishes Swichboard from being "just another well-designed Scandinavian SaaS." Magnus does not buy products from companies that sell to office workers. He buys Hilti drills, Bahco wrenches, Husqvarna saws, Snickers workwear, Iittala glassware (because his sambo bought it), Volvo lastbilar, Atlas Copco kompressorer. These products share a design philosophy that goes deeper than aesthetics:

**Form follows performance, not fashion.** A Hilti drill looks like a Hilti drill in 2002 and in 2026. The design serves the ergonomics, the heat dissipation, the dust ingress, the dropped-from-a-ladder durability. Aesthetics emerge from those decisions, not the other way around.

**Color signals function.** Hilti red. Husqvarna orange. Stihl orange-and-white. Bosch blue. These are not brand decoration — they are visibility colors. When you drop a tool in long grass or fog, you find it because the color was specifically chosen to be findable. Visibility colors are the original semantic colors.

**The user is presumed competent.** The interfaces are dense with information when information is needed. No hand-holding modals. The manual is in the box. The trust is implicit and reciprocal.

Swichboard borrows the philosophy: every visual choice in the product UI must be defensible on functional grounds first, beauty second. The orange isn't decorative — it's the color that tells Magnus, in poor light, in a kryputrymme, with one bar of 4G, that THIS is the tap-target that handles the akutsamtal. Beauty, in Verkstad, is what's left over when nothing dishonest remains.

## 3. The personas, in design terms

Each persona experiences a different surface of the brand. The system has to work for all four without becoming generic for any of them.

### 3.1 Magnus — _the buyer_, 47, VVS-företagare, Bromma

What Magnus encounters first: a Facebook ad in his VVS-grupp, a colleague's recommendation in a kafferast, a stand at Nordbygg. What needs to happen in the first three seconds of contact: he must see the product and immediately understand _"det här är gjort för mig — inte för en Södermalms-startup."_

The visual cues that achieve this:

- A photograph he can place himself inside — a panelbil dashboard with a phone in a magnetic holder, not an open-plan office with someone holding a MacBook.
- A headline written in his words ("_Du behöver inte stanna mitt i jobbet för att svara i telefon_") rather than the solution in startup words ("AI-driven voice automation for SMBs").
- A price visible without three clicks. Magnus does not "request a demo."
- A feeling of quiet, established competence — the same feeling he gets from an Ahlsell catalog or a Dahl-säljare, not from a VC-deck.

Inside the product, Magnus's primary interaction is the akutnotis on his phone, often glanced at one-handed while the other holds a 17mm rörnyckel. The design has to be readable in a single glance, single-handedly operable, and high-contrast enough to see in direct sunlight on a tak in juli or in the headlamp-yellow of a källare in november.

### 3.2 Lena — _the daily driver_, 44, kontorsadministratör, Magnus's sambo

Lena uses the dashboard as her morning ritual. She opens it before her second coffee. Her experience needs to feel less like "operating a tool" and more like "reading the morning paper." This is where the Pleo-influence is strongest: editorial calm, generous spacing, restrained color, type doing the work. The dashboard should feel like Lena could spend twelve minutes with it and finish her coffee in the same time. Not an alert console. A briefing.

When Lena clicks into a samtalsdetalj, the experience deepens — full transcript, audio player, structured data extracted by the AI, customer history. Density appears, but only when she has earned it. The interface respects that she is a competent administrator who knows what she is looking at; it does not patronize her with onboarding tooltips after the third use.

### 3.3 Alex — _the on-call_, 28, jourtekniker, every other week

Alex receives a notification in the middle of his evening — at home, possibly while putting his kids to bed. The akutnotis arrives on his lock screen. What he experiences in the next eight seconds determines whether he uses the product or works around it.

The design here borrows directly from the industrial-tool philosophy: high contrast, single-purpose surface, one obvious primary action. The signaloranje does the same job that hi-vis does on a roadway — it tells him, before he reads a word, what kind of message this is. The information hierarchy is rigid: who, where, what, how-soon. No second screen needed. One thumb, two seconds, decision made.

If Alex stops trusting the akutnotis — if it cries wolf even once a month — he stops opening it, and the product fails. The design must therefore restrain itself ferociously. Orange means orange. It's never used to "draw attention" to a feature update or a marketing message. The color is sacred.

### 3.4 Inger — _the invisible persona_, 71, the calling customer

Inger never sees the design at all. She experiences only the voice. But the brand promise is delivered in those 38 seconds: a calm, competent Swedish voice that takes her seriously, doesn't make her repeat herself, and leaves her with the certainty that someone is coming.

The design system must constantly remember Inger. Every visual decision Magnus or Lena makes must serve her experience indirectly — because her experience is the product. The dashboard exists to make sure her vattenläcka doesn't get lost. The brand exists so Magnus chooses Swichboard over a billigare svarstjänst, which means Inger gets answered when she calls.

This is the discipline: when in doubt, the design choice that helps Inger gets answered wins. Not the one that wins a Dribbble like.

## 4. Mood and atmosphere

Swichboard is:

**Daylight, not stage lighting.** Photography uses natural light — the cold blue-gray of a January morning in Bromma, the amber of a summer afternoon through a kitchen window in Skellefteå, the headlamp-yellow of a basement work light. Never the flat ringlight studio look of consumer SaaS. If the photo could have been shot in California, reshoot it.

**Quiet, not loud.** When Magnus opens the dashboard, the experience is calm. The signaloranje is rare enough that when it appears, attention follows. A constantly-alarming dashboard is one Magnus stops opening — and when Magnus stops opening it, Lena stops trusting it.

**Professional, not corporate.** Professional means: typography-led, content-respecting, informationally dense where appropriate. Corporate means: stock photography, hero illustrations of diverse cartoon people, gradient backgrounds, "thought leadership," the word "empower." Swichboard is the former, never the latter.

**Warm, not cute.** The brand has humor — it lives in the voice of the AI itself ("_kanon, då bokar jag in det_"), in the morning sammanfattning that says _"Du fångade tre akutjobb i natt — kosta dig själv en till kopp kaffe på det"_. The warmth comes from voice and copy, not from rounded illustrations and emoji.

**Swedish, not international.** Diacritics typeset correctly (å, ä, ö, never æ or accidentally ae). Months in lowercase ("22 maj" not "May 22"). Time in 24-hour ("14:32" never "2:32 PM"). Currency with non-breaking space and SEK postfix ("1 495 SEK", never "kr1,495" or "$152"). Svenska kvalitetsbokstäver. The brand has a passport, and it's blue.

## 5. Typography philosophy

Two type families do all the work. No third for "personality." No display font for landing pages and a different one for the product.

**Display: Söhne or Söhne Breit (or a closely related grotesque)** — used for headlines, hero copy, and the wordmark. The reference is Swiss-Swedish modernism: confident, geometric without being cold, slightly heavier than expected at large sizes (this is borrowed from Klarna's bias toward type that fills the space). Headlines are sized aggressively per the Klarna principle — when you can go to 96px, go to 96px.

**Body: Söhne (regular weight)** — used for everything else. Highly legible at 14–16px on phone screens at arm's length, in poor light. Tabular numerals enabled by default for time, currency, and phone numbers — Magnus reads them at a glance.

Two weights only in the product UI: Regular (400) and Medium (500). Bold is reserved for the wordmark and marketing display copy. This restraint is borrowed from the industrial tool sensibility — a Hilti drill doesn't have a "bold mode."

**Sentence case always.** Swedish convention, also Klarna and Pleo convention. "Boka in tid" not "Boka In Tid." The only exception: the wordmark itself.

## 6. Color philosophy

The palette is intentionally narrow. A wider palette would dilute the signal.

**Havsblå (#0A1F33)** — primary brand. Used for the wordmark, navigation, body type on light surfaces, primary buttons. Reads as serious, established, Nordic. Dark enough to feel substantial; not pure black, which would feel cold and corporate.

**Off-white / Linne (#F4EFE6)** — primary surface color, with a warm undertone (linne = linen). Borrowed from Pleo's preference for warm whites over clinical whites. The dashboard background. Marketing surface background. Ships with a cooler variant for product UI (#FAFAF7) where contrast against photography is needed.

**Signaloranje (#E25822)** — the one signal color. Reserved for: the primary CTA button, the emergency state, the wordmark accent dot, and one or two specifically branded marketing moments per year. Never used for hover states, link underlines, or "look at this new feature" decoration.

**Tallgrön (#2F5233)** — a single semantic green for confirmed bookings and positive states. The color of a Swedish pine forest, not the color of a bank app's success toast.

**Larmröd (#A82E2E)** — for genuinely critical states only (gas leak, AI failure, payment failure). Not used in marketing.

**Systemic grays** — five-step neutral ramp tuned warm (a hint of yellow in the gray, never blue). The dashboard's secondary text, dividers, disabled states.

That's the palette. Seven colors. Anyone designing in Verkstad who wants to add an eighth has to defend why.

## 7. Photography and imagery direction

This is the highest-leverage part of the brand. Get this right and Swichboard is recognizable from twenty meters; get it wrong and it looks like every other SaaS.

**Subjects.** Real Swedish hantverkare in real Swedish working environments. VVS-firmor in 1970s-built bostadsrättsföreningar. Elektriker in panelbilar. Snickare in newbuilds in Hökarängen. Plattsättare in renoveringsprojekt. Never models. Never staged "diverse young professional team" group shots.

**Light.** Natural, almost always. Morning light through kontorsfönster in Bromma. Headlamp-yellow in källare and kryputrymmen. Cold December afternoons. Midsommar sun. The light tells the viewer where in Sweden, and what time of year, the photograph is from — and that specificity is the brand.

**Objects.** Tools and trade objects are protagonists, not props. A Bahco skiftnyckel resting on a faktura. A iPad propped against a coffee cup at 06:30. An iPhone in a magnetic dashboard holder showing a Swichboard notification. Honest, used, scuffed. Nothing brand-new looking.

**Composition.** Borrowed from Pleo: the still-life sensibility. Objects are arranged but the arrangement looks accidental. Negative space is generous. The product screenshot is integrated into the physical scene, not floating against a gradient.

**What never appears in Swichboard photography:**
- Stock photography of any kind
- Models who are clearly not tradespeople
- Open-plan offices with bean bags
- Hands typing on laptops with an aspirational Pinterest workspace behind them
- Group brainstorm scenes with sticky notes
- 3D-rendered isometric scenes
- Sunsets, mountains, "freedom" imagery
- Anything that could appear on the homepage of a generic CRM tool

## 8. Motion and feel

Motion is restrained. The product is used in stressful contexts; bouncy animations read as unserious.

**Transitions are functional, not decorative.** A booked appointment slides into the calendar — yes. A modal fades in over 120ms — yes. The dashboard does not have a confetti animation when Magnus closes a high-value job; that's a consumer-app reflex and it's wrong here.

**Loading states show what's loading.** Never indeterminate spinners on screens longer than a half-second. Skeleton states that show the structure of the content arriving. Specifically: when the AI is mid-call, the live-call screen shows a calm pulsing indicator on the call card — the heartbeat of an active call, not a loading spinner.

**The akutnotis has its own motion language.** When a true emergency arrives on Magnus's phone, the notification animates differently than any other notification in the system: a single, attention-claiming pulse, then stillness. Not a continuous wobble. The first pulse signals; the stillness signals "this is now waiting on you."

## 9. Voice and copy

The brand's tone of voice is inseparable from the visual system. Three rules:

**Du, never ni.** Swedish second-person familiar. Always. Magnus and Lena use it; the AI uses it with their customers; the brand uses it with everyone. "Ni" reads as 1980s-banking and is wrong for this brand.

**Concrete over abstract.** "Boka tid med Magnus" beats "Schedule a service appointment." "Vattenläcka kl 14:32" beats "New incident detected." The product handles concrete jobs; the language stays concrete.

**Magnus's voice, not the corporation's voice.** When the brand has to say something — a confirmation toast, an empty state, an error message — it speaks the way Magnus speaks: rakt, varmt, kort. _"Det funkade. Bokningen ligger i kalendern."_ Not _"Your appointment has been successfully scheduled and added to your calendar."_

## 10. What Swichboard is NOT — the anti-brand

The negative space matters. These directions are explicitly rejected.

**Not Silicon Valley friendly.** No purple-pink gradients, no "Get started — it takes 30 seconds!" exclamation, no chatbot mascot, no isometric illustrations of cartoon plumbers, no growth-hacky "🎉 Welcome aboard!" copy.

**Not glassmorphism.** No frosted-glass cards, no cinematic backgrounds, no floating widgets. Magnus is not impressed and Lena finds it harder to read.

**Not minimalism for its own sake.** Pleo-style restraint applies to marketing surfaces. Inside the product, when a job-site emergency is unfolding, density is kindness. Show all the relevant info on one screen. Don't hide essential information behind progressive disclosure when seconds matter.

**Not generic Scandinavian.** "Clean lines, neutral palette, sans-serif type" is the global SaaS aesthetic now and reads as nothing in particular. The grid discipline, the signaloranje, the photography direction, and the Swedish-specific copy are what make Verkstad recognizable.

**Not aspirational lifestyle branding.** Magnus is not aspiring. He is competent and busy. The brand serves him; it does not invite him to imagine a better version of himself.

---

## 11. The brand promise the design has to keep

Every visual decision earns its place by answering one of these four questions in the affirmative:

1. Does this make Magnus more likely to trust us with his customers?
2. Does this make Lena's morning twelve minutes shorter?
3. Does this give Alex one fewer mistake when he's tired?
4. Does this make Inger feel — even though she never sees the brand — that the company on the other end of the line is competent enough to come fix her läcka?

If a design choice can't answer at least one of those affirmatively, it doesn't ship. This is the test. It is the only test that matters.
